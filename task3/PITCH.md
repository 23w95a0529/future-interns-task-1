---

## 1. Who is this for

Tumbler & Leaf is a neighbourhood filter coffee and chai house — the kind of place that runs on regulars, word of mouth, and a menu that hasn't changed much because it doesn't need to. Like a lot of great local spots, it doesn't currently have a proper website: maybe a Google listing, maybe an Instagram page, but nothing a first-time visitor can pull up in five seconds to answer basic questions.

This proposal uses Tumbler & Leaf as the example — the same site structure works for any café, salon, gym, boutique, or clinic that sells a clear set of things to a local crowd.

## 2. The problem this solves

Right now, someone deciding whether to visit has to guess:

- **"Is it open right now?"** — no listed hours, or hours buried in a review from two years ago.
- **"What do they actually serve, and how much does it cost?"** — no menu without walking in or messaging on Instagram and waiting for a reply.
- **"Where exactly is it, and how do I get there?"** — a pin on a map with no landmark context.
- **"Is this place actually good?"** — no visible signal beyond scattered reviews.
- **"Can I just message them instead of calling?"** — most young customers would rather WhatsApp than phone.

Every one of these is a small moment of friction. Small moments of friction are exactly where a potential customer decides to go somewhere else instead — usually a competitor who *does* have this information one tap away.

## 3. What the website does about it

| Problem | What's on the site |
|---|---|
| No visible hours/location | Hours, full address, and an embedded map, right on the contact section |
| No menu online | A full categorized menu with prices — Filter Coffee, Chai, Tiffin, Snacks |
| No easy way to order or ask a question | A floating WhatsApp button on every page, plus a contact form |
| No sense of the place before visiting | A gallery section and an "our story" section that gives the business a personality, not just a transaction |
| Doesn't work well on phones | Fully responsive — most customers will find this on a phone, not a laptop |

## 4. Why this matters for the business, in plain terms

- **Looks credible.** A business with a clean, working website reads as more established — even at the same price point as a competitor without one.
- **Cuts down repetitive questions.** Right now, "what are your hours," "where are you," and "what's on the menu" are probably answered one DM at a time. A website answers them for free, all day.
- **Makes the WhatsApp order path effortless.** The floating WhatsApp button means a customer never has to save a number or search for the Instagram page — one tap, and they're already messaging.
- **Gives the business something to link to.** Instagram bio, Google Business listing, QR code on the table — all of these currently have nowhere good to point. Now they have one clear destination.
- **Costs nothing to keep running.** As built, this is a static site — no hosting fees beyond a few dollars a year for a domain, and no monthly software subscription.

## 5. What's included in this build

- A homepage with all key sections: hero, menu, about, gallery, testimonials, and contact
- A four-category, fully priced menu, ready to edit
- Mobile-first design — tested down to small phone screens
- Google Maps embed and a click-to-WhatsApp button
- A working contact form (needs one 5-minute setup step — see README)
- Placeholder photo tiles clearly marked, ready to swap for real photos

## 6. What the business would need to do next

1. **Share real photos** — of the counter, the food, the seating — to replace the placeholder tiles and the "about" photo.
2. **Confirm real details** — exact address, phone number, opening hours, and menu prices, to replace the example data used in this build.
3. **Approve the contact form email** — a one-time click to confirm the inbox that should receive messages (see README for the exact step).
4. **Pick a domain name** (e.g. `tumblerandleaf.in`) and a place to host it — this can be free (GitHub Pages) or a few dollars a year (Netlify, Vercel, or standard hosting).

None of this requires the owner to learn anything technical — steps 1–3 are just providing information, and step 4 can be handled entirely by the developer.
